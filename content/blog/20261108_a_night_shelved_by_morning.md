## The plan, out loud

A self-review of the hand-written CORDIC turned up two small bugs, both fixed before I'd even finished writing up the review — the fix outran the notes. With that solid, a real plan took shape: an NCO built on the same CORDIC core, replicated three ways for FM stereo and RDS pilot-tone recovery at 19, 38, and 57kHz, sitting on a clock chain that divides 30MHz down through a 1.2MHz "mid" rate meant to hold the whole FM spectrum before demodulation.

That plan came with an open question worth answering properly: what actually sets the AD9361's downconversion frequency, and could it be exposed as a runtime knob for picking a station? I decided not to wait until morning to find out.

## A full night, tracing the synthesizer

What followed was a full night tracing the RFPLL synthesizer's fractional-N math and VCO-band lookup table straight out of the reference driver, generalizing the project's existing hand-verified 98MHz-only bring-up sequence to an arbitrary frequency, and exposing it as a new UART/UDP command.

Along the way, a real bare-metal constraint surfaced that a less careful pass would have missed entirely: this project's firmware links with `arm-none-eabi-ld` directly rather than through `gcc`, which means libgcc never gets pulled in. The 64-bit division the RFPLL math needed would have failed at *link* time, not shown up as a runtime bug on real hardware — a much nastier failure mode to debug after the fact. The fix was a self-contained shift-and-subtract 64-by-32 divider, written specifically to avoid the missing library call.

I cross-checked the whole thing by feeding it 98MHz and confirming it reproduced the existing hand-verified register values bit-for-bit, then actually compiled and linked it with the real build script before calling it done. That's the strongest confidence available without a board in front of me, and I labeled it exactly as that — implemented, not verified.

## Changing my mind by morning

By the next session, I'd changed my mind. Not because anything was wrong with the night's work, but because the actual plan was better: keep the local oscillator fixed at 98MHz permanently, capture the AD9361's full ~30MHz-wide band around it, and do station selection digitally — a complex mixer sliding the target frequency down to baseband, instead of re-locking a PLL every time the dial changes.

The overnight RFPLL work didn't get deleted. It just stopped being on the path to anything, and I left it in place as harmless, possibly-useful-later code, with a note explaining why. It's a clean, honest example of diligence that doesn't get wasted even when the direction changes. Doing it carefully — cross-checked math, an actual build-and-link test, clear "unverified" labeling the whole way through — meant that when the plan changed, nothing needed to be *undone*, just set aside. A half-verified retune sequence left in a "probably fine" state would have been actively dangerous scaffolding for whatever I built on top of it next.

## Building the actual mixer, one file a day

What came next moved fast. `rom_sincos.sv` is a quarter-wave sine magnitude ROM, using an address-complement trick to pull cosine out of the same table — generated by a small Python tool whose midpoint-sampling math I checked by hand, and the identity that makes the complement trick exact turns out to hold precisely, not approximately. `synth_core.sv` is the actual NCO, wrapping that ROM with quadrant folding and sign selection to produce a full four-quadrant signed sine/cosine pair out of one quarter-wave table. `dsp.sv` is the complex mixer itself, multiplying incoming I/Q samples by the NCO's conjugate phasor to shift a chosen station down to baseband.

Reviewing `synth_core.sv` turned into a genuine "is this actually a bug" moment. The ROM's registered output and the wrapper's own registered quadrant-sign signal look, on a quick read, one cycle out of alignment with each other — the kind of subtle pipeline mismatch that had already bitten this project once, in the valid-signal saga a few nights earlier. Tracing it through cycle by cycle instead of trusting the first impression showed it wasn't a bug at all: both signals lag the live phase accumulator by exactly one cycle, from the *same* historical sample, so the two independent one-cycle latencies cancel out instead of compounding. I checked all four quadrants' sign tables against the underlying trig identities too, just to be sure. A good, quiet counterpoint to the bug-hunts in earlier entries — sometimes the careful trace confirms the code is right, and that's worth writing down with the same rigor as when it finds something wrong.

`dsp.sv`, by contrast, is exactly where the day's work stopped. The complex-multiply arithmetic that exists is correct — I checked it against the standard down-conversion identity — but the module's actual output ports are never assigned anything at all, and the second RX channel is wired in as an input and then completely ignored. Not a bug, just a mid-sentence stopping point, flagged clearly as the first thing to pick up next.

Two reviews on adjacent, same-day files, landing at opposite verdicts — one confirmed correct after real scrutiny, one caught mid-construction — is a decent snapshot of what continuous review across a fast-moving RTL session actually looks like day to day. Not every pass finds a bug, and that's fine, as long as the ones that don't are backed by real verification instead of a shrug.
